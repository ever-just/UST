## Q1. What’s the relevant IP/trademark/copyright that’s applicable?

For Kinetic’s products (FlexiStep and KneeComfort Pro), the most relevant IP types are:

- **Utility patents**
  - Protect how a device works: functional features, methods, sensor integration, mechanisms.
- **Design patents**
  - Protect the **ornamental appearance** of the device: overall shape, contours, geometry.
- **Trade dress / trademarks**
  - Trade dress: distinctive overall look and feel that signals a particular source.
  - Trademarks: names, logos, brand identifiers.
- **Copyright** (secondary but still relevant)
  - Protects original **text, diagrams, manuals, software UI, marketing materials**.

For this scenario, the **core legal risk** is around **utility and design patents** (plus possibly trade dress), because Kinetic’s products resemble existing Stryker/BoneFoam devices in both function and look.

## Q2. What’s Stryker’s/BoneFoam’s current IP status relevant to our products?

- **Stryker**
  - Large portfolio of **utility and design patents** on implants and related tools, including the **Infinity Total Ankle System**.
  - Patents likely cover:
    - Component geometry and alignment.
    - How forces are distributed across components.
    - How parts interact mechanically.
  - Even though FlexiStep is **not an implant**, copying similar **geometries and load‑bearing ideas** can still raise design/utility concerns.

- **BoneFoam**
  - Holds patents on **foam positioning devices**:
    - Specific shapes, cut‑outs, angles, modular pieces.
    - Ways to support limbs and maintain certain positions.
  - KneeComfort Pro being **“almost identical”** in shape and modularity threatens those patent boundaries.

For your project, you can treat both companies as **IP‑intensive incumbents** whose patents and designs are closely related to what Kinetic is building.

## Q3. What’s the likelihood of dispute (legal conflict history)?

- **General pattern in medical devices**:
  - Large companies like Stryker often **enforce key patents** when competitors introduce products that appear derivative.
  - They may start with **demand letters, licensing pressure**, and only escalate if needed.
- **For specialized companies like BoneFoam**:
  - Core product patents are often their **main assets**, so they tend to protect them more aggressively.

Given:

- Kinetic’s internal notes explicitly referencing Stryker’s designs as “industry benchmarks”, and
- KneeComfort Pro’s near‑identical look to BoneFoam devices,

…it is reasonable for your analysis to assume a **non‑trivial to high risk** of dispute if Kinetic launches without design changes or legal risk‑mitigation.

## Q8. Does using a website for patient uploads reduce consent friction compared to physician data?

- **Physician‑held data approach**:
  - Raises HIPAA and data‑ownership questions.
  - Patients may never have clearly consented to their data being shared with a company like Kinetic.

- **Patient‑uploaded data (website/app)**:
  - Allows Kinetic to obtain **direct consent** from patients.
  - Consent forms can clearly state:
    - Who is collecting the data (Kinetic).
    - How it will be used (research, product improvement, etc.).
  - This can **reduce friction** and legal ambiguity around consent **if done properly**.

But:

- It does **not eliminate** privacy, security, or regulatory risk.
- Kinetic must still ensure:
  - Clear, understandable consent language.
  - Robust security and privacy controls.
